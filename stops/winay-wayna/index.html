What is Wiñayhuayna (Winay Wayna)?

Wiñayhuayna — also written Winay Wayna, Wiñay Wayna, or Winaywayna — is both a major Inca archaeological site and the name trekkers use for the usual Camp 3 on the classic Inca Trail. The Quechua is commonly glossed as “forever young.”

Our tour data lists the camp/site area near 8,812 ft (2,685 m) — much lower than the high passes of Days 2–3, in warmer cloud-forest climate with views into deep valleys.

Ruins: terraces and water

A short walk from the campsite, the ruins cascade down the hillside:

  • Agricultural terraces stepped into the slope — among the most photogenic farming earthworks on the trail.
  • Ceremonial baths / fountains — stone channels moving spring water through carved basins. Popular trek sources describe a small set of ritual baths (counts of five or six appear often) plus longer channel runs. Counts change with how walls are tallied — we describe an elaborate water system rather than locking a single sacred number without a primary archaeological citation.
  • Residential and ceremonial structures arranged around plazas on different levels.

Unlike a quick viewpoint stop, Wiñayhuayna rewards slow walking: water sound, terrace geometry, and the sense of a living support site for the Machu Picchu network — not a single temple on a summit.

The campsite (Camp 3)

On the classic 4-day itinerary, most groups overnight at the Wiñay Wayna campsite when space allows. It is the default last camp before Machu Picchu, but it can fill — some itineraries use higher alternatives such as Phuyupatamarca. Distance to Inti Punku is often cited around ~4–4.8 km / ~3 miles (operator figures vary). Tent areas are frequently described as engineered platforms on the order of ~125 m² of prepared ground with retaining structure.

Facilities are relatively developed for the trail:

  • Tent platforms on engineered terraces / retaining structure
  • Toilets; cold showers when water systems allow
  • Sometimes a small kiosk for snacks, water, or basic drinks (availability varies by season and stock)

It is also one of the busier social nights: groups converge, sleep is short, and wake-up for Day 4 is very early.

GPS note. In the Inca Trail 3D dataset, Camp 3 / Wiñayhuayna is a key waypoint before the final push to Inti Punku and Machu Picchu. The 3D tour stop labeled for Camp 3 corresponds to this sector of the track.

Why the 3–4 AM alarm exists

Day 4 is short in distance but strict in timing. Groups leave in the dark to queue at the checkpoint and reach Inti Punku (the Sun Gate) near sunrise when weather cooperates. From camp to gate is a steady hike on Inca stone — not a casual stroll — so “sleep in” is not on the menu if you want the classic arrival.

Practical tips for Wiñayhuayna

  • Pack a headlamp and spare batteries the night before; test it at camp.
  • Visit the ruins while you still have daylight on Day 3 — do not assume you will have time in the morning.
  • Noise and early calls are normal; earplugs help light sleepers.
  • Fill water and prep day-pack before sleep so the scramble at 3:30 AM is calmer.
  • Keep a warm layer handy; pre-dawn cloud forest can feel colder than the afternoon.
  • Respect ruin boundaries — stay on allowed paths with your guide.

Notes on sources

Camp logistics (default last camp, facilities, approximate distance to the Sun Gate) follow widely published operator guides. Architectural bath counts vary by source; this page favors cautious language over a single contested number.

How it fits the story of the trail

After high passes and cliffside sites like Sayacmarca and Phuyupatamarca, Wiñayhuayna is a return to lush slopes and dense stonework. It is the emotional “last night on the trail” for most trekkers — tired legs, early alarm, and Machu Picchu finally close.
